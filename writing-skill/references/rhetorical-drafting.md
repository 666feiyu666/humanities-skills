# Rhetorical drafting between argument and prose

## Purpose

Convert a writer-confirmed direction into prose that a particular reader can enter and follow. Use this reference for a full article or substantial new passage, continuation, or revision. It supplies the missing layer between a line of argument and sentences; it does not choose the writer's thesis or replace source verification.

Do not use it to create another required project artifact. Keep the reader path internal unless the writer asks to review rhetorical design or a consequential rhetorical choice would alter the article's substantive action.

## Keep three layers distinct

1. **Line of argument**: what the writer is committed to saying, why movements belong together, which material supports them, and which boundaries must hold.
2. **Reader path**: where the reader begins, how the writer invites, orients, surprises, reassures, challenges, or repositions them, and why each movement becomes relevant when it does.
3. **Prose**: the actual paragraphs, sentences, examples, questions, transitions, rhythm, and voice that realize both.

Do not collapse these layers. An argument node is not a paragraph. An outline arrow is not a connective. A source note is not a sentence. A boundary in the line of argument is not automatically a disclaimer in the prose.

## Establish the rhetorical situation

Before substantial prose, recover the smallest useful rhetorical situation from the writer's brief, confirmed line of argument, existing prose, and revisions:

- what the reader likely knows, assumes, feels, or finds difficult at the point of entry;
- what relationship the writer wants with the reader: explaining alongside them, testifying from experience, challenging a commonplace, correcting a misconception, offering judgment, or another supplied stance;
- what the passage should let the reader notice, reconsider, feel, or carry forward;
- what reliable voice evidence the writer has supplied through their own wording and revisions.

Do not invent a generic audience to fill these fields. The writer's latest revision is stronger evidence than a general preference such as `natural`, `academic`, or `accessible`.

Treat rhetorical function separately from propositional content. A question may recall a shared commonplace, create curiosity, pace an explanation, anticipate resistance, or reorient the discussion without becoming the governing question that the article promises to answer. A familiar claim used for identification is not automatically a premise in the argument. Determine what the move does to the writer-reader relationship before auditing it as a literal claim.

## Derive an internal reader path

Before drafting each substantial passage, determine internally:

1. **Entry position**: where the previous passage has left the reader.
2. **Invitation or pressure**: what makes this material worth attending to now.
3. **Movement**: what sequence of recognition, explanation, complication, or judgment the reader should experience.
4. **Resistance**: what confusion, objection, abstraction, or distance needs anticipation.
5. **Exit position**: what the reader should now understand and why the next movement is available.

This is not a new outline to display or a fixed five-part paragraph template. Use only the distinctions that help with the actual passage. An explicit enumeration can be the right way to reassure a reader facing unfamiliar material. A single example may carry several argument nodes. One node may need several differently shaped paragraphs. Preserve the writer's intended structure rather than replacing it with a generic preference for implicitness.

## Compose from rhetorical movement

- Write from the reader path, then use the line of argument as a constraint and coverage check. Do not generate one paragraph per outline bullet merely because each bullet exists.
- Enact a rhetorical choice instead of announcing the planning decision behind it. If a familiar example should open a concept, begin with the example; do not tell the reader that the example is a useful entry point.
- Let questions do their actual work. Do not force a rhetorical invitation to carry a causal inference, and do not weaken a genuine argumentative question into decoration.
- Use transitions to register a real change in the reader's position, the question, or the level of analysis. Do not translate arrows in the outline into `therefore`, `however`, or their equivalents.
- Keep argumentative boundaries in the background when the prose does not create the misunderstanding they guard against. State a qualification when a reader needs it, not merely because the outline records it.
- Allow deliberate first-person presence, direct address, hesitation, explicit enumeration, metadiscourse, repetition, humor, and uneven emphasis when they establish the intended relationship or pace. Do not add them cosmetically.
- Move between concrete material and abstraction at the pace of the reader's developing understanding. Avoid presenting every source point at one uniform level of summary.
- Let paragraphs remain open when the next paragraph completes their work. Do not give every paragraph a topic sentence, catalogue of support, and polished local conclusion.

## Use voice evidence functionally

When the writer revises prose, infer what the change does rhetorically as well as linguistically. Useful evidence may include:

- beginning from a shared scene or commonplace before naming an abstraction;
- asking a question to invite rather than interrogate the reader;
- using `I` or `we` to locate a judgment;
- preserving a pause, aside, or hesitation so the writer remains present;
- preferring explicit enumeration when explanation would otherwise feel intimidating;
- letting an evaluation emerge from reading experience instead of announcing it in advance;
- varying paragraph length because movements need different amounts of space.

Carry a reliable function into the remaining prose without copying the same surface marker. One meaningful hesitation does not authorize filler throughout; one useful three-part sequence does not require every section to have three parts.

## Audit the realized reader path

After drafting, conduct this pass separately from the line-of-argument comparison:

- What question or expectation does the opening actually create?
- What relationship does the prose establish between writer and reader?
- Why does each substantial movement arrive at that point rather than merely after the previous outline item?
- Are examples being used, or is the prose explaining why they would be useful?
- Do questions perform the intended rhetorical function without creating accidental argumentative promises?
- Does the passage create progress, or only demonstrate that every outline point has been covered?
- Do abstraction, scene, explanation, and judgment have enough variation in pace and proportion?
- Does the ending complete the rhetorical action actually performed rather than recite the outline or add generic uplift?

Repair rhetorical failure in the prose. Update the line of argument only when the repair requires a substantive change to the governing question, central judgment, concept, relation, evidence selection, or boundary.

## Common conversion failures

- **Planning language in prose**: the draft says that an example is a good entry point instead of entering through it.
- **Node-to-paragraph transcription**: every outline point becomes a paragraph with the same claim-explanation-summary shape.
- **Arrow-to-connective transcription**: logical or spatial links in the outline become repetitive transition words without reader-visible movement.
- **Boundary-to-disclaimer transcription**: qualifications appear before the prose has created any reason for the reader to need them.
- **Rhetorical over-literalization**: a question or commonplace used to build identification is treated as a thesis, causal claim, or promise that must be defended.
- **Anti-formula overcorrection**: explicit enumeration, direct address, or metadiscourse chosen by the writer is removed merely because it can be formulaic in other texts.
- **Surface voice imitation**: a pause, question, or colloquial marker is repeated while the underlying writer-reader relationship remains unchanged.
