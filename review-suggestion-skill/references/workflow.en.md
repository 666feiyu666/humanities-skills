# English collaboration for review suggestions

Use English for the review unless the writer explicitly chooses another collaboration language. Capability, evidence, and ownership rules remain in [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Preserve the draft's and each source's language independently of the review language.

## Establish the basis without a questionnaire

Read the current draft or requested passage, including the ending when assessing its argumentative destination. Use the writer's stated genre, audience, discipline, and concerns. When these are inferable, proceed; for example, a public book review should not be graded as a research article. If only an excerpt is available, do not claim to have assessed the entire piece.

Use the teacher/researcher perspective to test concepts, evidence, and inference, and the intended reader's perspective to test what becomes understandable or newly visible. Do not announce a fictional professional identity. State a domain or audience assumption only when it affects the recommendations.

## Select the judgments that matter

Reconstruct the text's actual question and progression before judging it. Apply the selected disciplinary lens to particular passages. Separate a supported error from a source check, and both from an editorial preference. A third-party review can suggest questions worth testing; its approval or criticism does not settle them.

For example, if a draft explains distinctions carefully but ends with a general warning, compare what the body establishes with what the ending actually claims. Suggest bringing the conclusion into line with the writer's existing argument, without choosing a new thesis for them. If a proposed correction concerns a theorist's classification, check the original hierarchy and attribution before calling the draft wrong. Neither example is a required topic for every review.

Reader experience is more than fluent wording. Ask whether a reader can see the stakes of a distinction or how an example supports an inference. Recommend a concrete case only when it would perform an identifiable job; do not invent one or treat a hypothetical case as observed evidence. Do not insist that every public essay become a story, or that every book review disagree with its subject.

## Shape the response

Use one to six numbered points in total, fewer when sufficient or when requested. Integrate any overall judgment into the first relevant point and any final priority into the relevant recommendation. Do not add a seventh conclusion, separate scorecard, source-warning section, or nested list of extra tasks.

For each point, connect a located observation to its consequence and a concrete revision direction in ordinary prose. For instance, “The paragraph beginning ‘…’ treats two different claims as interchangeable. This leaves the reader unsure which claim the example supports. Distinguish them here before using the example.” This is a reasoning pattern, not a required sentence template or set of labels.

Order by consequence for the current text. Often conceptual or evidential problems precede structural or stylistic ones, but the writer's stated concern may justify another order. Mention a particular strength when it tells the writer what to preserve; do not add a praise quota. Avoid stock verdicts such as “excellent overall, but room to improve,” inflated publication assurances, and unrequested numerical scores.

Keep each point compact. A short quotation or precise paragraph locator is usually enough. Explain related edits together; split unrelated judgments only if they deserve a place within the six-point limit. Do not turn this review into a rewrite. Any illustrative new framing remains a model proposal for author review.

Return proposals, not the writer's settled conclusions. Do not edit or save files unless requested. An accepted recommendation can then be developed through the appropriate reading, writing, or prose-editing workflow, without assuming that acceptance of one point approves the entire review.
