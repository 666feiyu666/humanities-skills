# Selecting disciplinary standards

These are selectable review methods, not a rubric to reproduce in the answer or an exhaustive map of disciplines. Read and use only the entry or entries relevant to the text. Preserve the writer's actual question, genre, and audience. A disciplinary term becomes useful only when it changes a judgment about a particular passage.

## Philosophy and theoretical argument, including philosophy of technology

Test whether concepts keep their scope across the argument; distinguish definitions, explanatory claims, and normative judgments. Check necessary versus sufficient conditions, levels within a classification, and whether an attributed position belongs to the source or to someone the source discusses. Distinguish reconstructing a thinker from extending or disagreeing with them. Ask whether an ending preserves the distinctions established in the body. Do not replace a specific conceptual problem with a generic ethical warning, or assume that presenting a warning is itself a defect.

## Science and technology studies (STS)

Trace the relation proposed between material arrangements, institutions, organizational needs, and particular actors. Ask whose ability to decide, act, or refuse is at stake, rather than assuming a single actor called “humanity.” Distinguish local operational control from influence over a system's aims. Keep a hypothetical development path distinct from a demonstrated social mechanism, and check whether alternatives are merely asserted or made intelligible. Do not force every text to cover all of these issues, or assume that technical requirements alone explain an arrangement or that social choices can remove every material constraint.

## Anthropology and social inquiry

Check how situated accounts support the scale of the claim. Distinguish participants' terms, the writer's analytical categories, and a reviewer's inference. Ask whether a local observation is being generalized, whether a causal claim has support beyond description, and whose perspective is absent when that absence affects the argument. Do not demand representativeness from every ethnographic example or treat an illustrative anecdote as population evidence. Recommend added context or voices only when the writer has a lawful source route and a clear argumentative need.

## Rhetoric, semiotics, narrative, and literary interpretation

Select the relevant method rather than treating these fields as interchangeable. In rhetorical analysis, test how claims about persuasion relate to audience and situation. In semiotic analysis, check how the proposed sign relation is supported. In narrative or literary interpretation, connect claims to wording, form, sequence, and viewpoint. Across these approaches, distinguish textual effect, interpretive inference, authorial intention, and reception evidence. A reader's plausible response is not proof of an author's intention or every audience's experience.

## Historical interpretation

Check chronology, source provenance, and the relation between contemporary categories and later interpretation. Distinguish what a document records from what the writer infers, and whether a change over time is evidenced or assumed. Test explanatory claims against the temporal and geographical scale actually covered. Do not demand exhaustive background; identify only the missing context that changes the present claim. A present-day comparison can be useful if its limits remain visible.

## Design and analysis of designed arrangements

Check the relation among the stated need, intended users, design choices, and consequences. Distinguish intention, a proposed mechanism, a prototype or demonstration, and observed use. Ask which constraints and value choices the argument makes visible, and whether claims about effectiveness or accessibility are supported at the stated scale. Do not assume novelty establishes value or require a full design evaluation for an interpretive essay about a designed object.

## Other or intersecting fields

Derive a focused lens from the field and genre the writer supplies: what is the object of inquiry, what would count as evidence, which distinction changes the inference, and what would make the conclusion too broad? State a consequential limit if these cannot be established. Use a second field only when it materially changes the reading; do not expand into a multi-disciplinary report or invent a specialist consensus.
