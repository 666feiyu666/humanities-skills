# Speaking Skill

## Purpose

Turn established material into speech that a real audience can follow and that the speaker can deliver naturally. Preserve the author's claims, uncertainty, examples, and voice while changing structure and wording where oral delivery requires it.

This is a lightweight speaking workflow. Do not create a production pipeline, media project, or presentation system unless the user separately asks for one.

## Language Routing

Before substantive work, select and read exactly one collaboration workflow: [English](references/workflow.en.md) or [Simplified Chinese](references/workflow.zh-CN.md). Follow an explicit language request first; otherwise use the language of the speaker's current substantive request. The selected workflow governs collaboration and user-facing labels; determine the delivery language separately from the language of source material. Load both only for translation, comparison, or a bilingual audit.

## Establish the speaking situation

Infer from the request when possible, and otherwise establish only the missing details that materially change the result:

- source material or already chosen message;
- audience and their likely background;
- occasion and desired effect;
- target duration;
- language, tone, and delivery constraints.

Do not require a formal brief when the request and supplied material already answer these questions.

## Adapt for oral delivery

Build one audible through-line rather than reproducing the written document section by section.

- Make the opening establish the situation, question, or promise quickly.
- Prefer sentences that can be understood on first hearing.
- Make transitions and changes of direction audible.
- Retain necessary conceptual precision and qualifications.
- Use examples, repetition, signposting, and pauses only when they help this audience follow the thought.
- Estimate timing from the actual script or outline and identify sections that are likely to overrun.

Do not manufacture anecdotes, evidence, quotations, or personal experiences. Mark any missing factual support or author decision instead of smoothing over it.

## Build a slide-supported delivery in stages

When the user wants an end-to-end slide-supported delivery, distinguish three stages. The stages protect the difference between the argument visible on screen, the substantive talk delivered behind it, and the final visual treatment.

### Stage 1: use the deck as a structural outline

Build the audible through-line and a concise slide storyboard. When the user wants an actual file at this stage, hand the storyboard to the presentation workflow to materialize a plainly formatted, editable working deck. Treat that deck as a paginated version of the storyboard and a thinking surface rather than a designed final artifact.

Default an actual stage-one deck to one slide title plus concise hierarchical bullets or short textual lines. Preserve indentation, sequence, contrast, and takeaway statements as text. Do not translate a comparison into columns, a sequence into a flow diagram, or a conceptual hierarchy into boxes merely because those relationships could be visualized. Even neutral cards, panels, process arrows, and spatial compositions are preliminary visual-design decisions. Use them at stage 1 only when the user explicitly wants to test a spatial structure rather than a textual storyboard.

Use only neutral default formatting needed to read the textual hierarchy. Do not select or apply a presentation theme, decorative palette, imagery, branded chrome, refined typographic system, animation, aesthetic diagram treatment, or other visual polish. Record visual ideas as notes for stage 3 instead of implementing them now.

For each proposed slide, distinguish as useful:

- the slide's function in the through-line;
- the minimal content the audience should see;
- the speaker's responsibility behind the slide;
- the transition and estimated time;
- source status, visual intent, or an unresolved author decision when it materially affects later work.

Do not make the projected slide a transcript of the speech. Review the working deck as a whole for sequence, hierarchy, pacing, cognitive load, redundancy, and whether each slide has a clear job. Approval at this stage settles a usable but provisional working structure, not the final content lock and not permission to beautify the deck.

### Stage 2: develop the substantive talk behind the deck

Develop the material that need not appear on screen: explanation, evidence, examples, qualifications, transitions, and the requested degree of scripting or speaker notes. Estimate timing from this actual oral material.

Test the substantive talk against the working deck. Revise slide order, titles, visible content, or transitions as much as the developed talk requires; keep the screen concise rather than copying new substance onto it. Continue using the neutral working format throughout this stage. Do not start visual design merely because individual slides appear stable. Return unresolved source claims to reading and unresolved central choices to thinking.

Content lock occurs only when the visible deck structure and the substantive oral material work together and the user confirms they are sufficiently stable. Record the locked slide order, minimal visible copy, substantive talk or notes, sources and asset constraints, visual intent, and any wording or claims that must not change silently. Until this gate is passed, presentation automation may edit the structural working deck but must not beautify it.

### Stage 3: polish the visual presentation

Only after content lock, hand the working deck and locked content packet to the relevant presentation workflow. It may implement the deck in PowerPoint, Google Slides, Keynote, Beamer, or another requested medium and refine layout, typography, imagery, and visual hierarchy. Do not pre-build a design system or partially polish the deck during stages 1 or 2. Visual production must not change the argument, evidence, or speaker-screen division silently; if it exposes a substantive problem, return that decision to stage 2.

After the polished deck is produced, return to oral adaptation when requested: reconcile the actual slides with speaker notes, transitions, timing, cue cards, and rehearsal needs.

If the user explicitly requests an autonomous end-to-end pass, Codex may proceed through all three stages without pausing, but it must still complete stage 2 and establish a provisional internal content freeze before beginning stage-3 visual work. The resulting deck and speaking materials remain review drafts; internal checks do not close the author review gate or make the content author-confirmed.

## Choose the smallest useful output

Match the artifact to the request. Useful outputs include:

- a timed speaking outline;
- a concise slide storyboard or structural working-deck handoff;
- a substantive speaker-screen plan or talk track;
- a content-locked handoff for presentation production;
- a full or partial oral script;
- cue cards or speaker prompts;
- a rehearsal plan with pacing and emphasis notes;
- likely audience questions and answer-preparation prompts;
- a delivery review of an existing script.

Do not automatically produce all formats.

## Preserve boundaries

For general learning, public expression, teaching, podcasts, or video narration, this skill may own the oral adaptation.

For an academic conference, defense, or research presentation, let `research-publishing`, when it owns the project, establish the scholarly content and claim hierarchy. After that content is sufficiently stable, this skill may plan oral wording, timing, delivery, rehearsal, and the relationship between screen and speech without changing the substantive argument.

This skill owns the delivery structure, substantive oral adaptation, and relationship between screen and speech. It does not itself create, render, or visually style slides. It may coordinate a presentation workflow to materialize a provisional structural deck in stage 1 and a polished deck in stage 3 without allowing that workflow to change the argument silently.

Return unresolved source claims to the relevant reading workflow and unresolved substantive choices to the relevant thinking workflow.

## Require author review of speaking artifacts

Treat every Codex-produced speaking outline, storyboard, talk track, script, cue card set, rehearsal plan, or substantial revision as a review draft until the speaker inspects it. Ask them to check:

- whether the message, claims, examples, and deliberate omissions remain theirs;
- whether audience assumptions and explanatory additions are acceptable;
- whether the wording is natural for them to say aloud;
- whether timing, emphasis, transitions, and screen-speech division fit the actual occasion.

The speaker may accept the artifact unchanged, revise it, request revision, or reject it. Apply their corrections to the active artifact and preserve them across related slides, notes, and cue cards. Do not treat content as author-confirmed or delivery-ready before this review. Internal timing checks, rehearsal heuristics, or an autonomous end-to-end request do not substitute for the speaker's final inspection.

## Return results

Return the current-stage artifact first and state whether the work is at structural approval, content lock, visual production, or rehearsal. Identify only the decisions that prevent the next gate. Then add the delivery information that helps the next use: estimated duration, deliberate omissions or compressions, passages needing author confirmation, and one practical rehearsal focus when relevant.
